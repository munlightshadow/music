From the Compilers

Today labor migration to Russia is, without exaggeration, a lifeline in a sea of life’s hardships for many citizens of the post-Soviet countries. Although in the years since the collapse of the USSR the new independent states — the former Union republics — have achieved certain successes in market reforms and have created conditions for the development of business and entrepreneurial activity, the situation on the labor market, especially in the countries of Central Asia, continues to be difficult. Under these conditions, one way out of the situation that has taken shape is the mass labor migration of the republics’ working-age population abroad. The absence of a visa regime makes it easier for labor migrants to enter and stay in Russia, and their remittances play a significant role in reducing poverty in the countries of the CIS. In addition, labor migration contributes to young people’s self-realization and constructive activity, and also to their acquisition of work skills, useful knowledge, new occupations, and experience of relations in a variety of life situations. Labor migration also has many positive aspects for the Russian Federation. Thanks to migrants, entire sectors of the economy develop to a certain extent: construction, agriculture, trade, services, and others. According to forecasts, under conditions of the dynamic development of Russia’s economy, the need for labor migrants will grow still greater in the near term. But at the same time labor migration, in the course of which relations take shape among people who have grown up in different sociocultural conditions, in different linguistic and behavioral environments, and who differ from one another in their views, convictions, and beliefs, cannot fail to be a source of a whole series of problems. And the most acute problem associated with labor migration is

the sociocultural adaptation of labor migrants. It is on the resolution of this question, which is organically connected with an important problem of our time — interethnic relations — that both the comfortable stay of labor migrants and the friendly, tolerant, and well-disposed attitude of the native population toward newcomers depend. The undoubted importance and urgency of this question is shown by the fact that Russia has adopted a special law “On the Social and Cultural Adaptation and Integration of Foreign Citizens in the Russian Federation,” in accordance with which centers for the sociocultural adaptation and integration of labor migrants have been created and are being created throughout the country. One of the main areas of activity of these centers is teaching labor migrants the Russian language and the history, culture, and legislation of Russia. In addition, this law provides for information campaigns aimed at forming a positive image of the labor migrant and strengthening interethnic accord in Russian society. On the degree of literacy, education, and general culture of young people — and the overwhelming majority of labor migrants are young people — depend not only their successful socialization in Russian society but also, to a known extent, the prospects for development and the future of the CIS countries. For young people, a working trip to Russia — one of the powerful centers of world science and education — should become not only an opportunity to improve their material situation but also a good occasion (a help) to join advanced culture, knowledge, and new technologies. It is perfectly obvious that however numerous the centers for the retraining of labor migrants may be, covering hundreds of thousands of people with instruction within any foreseeable period is a difficult matter. And if one takes into account that labor migrants, before entering the Russian Federation, are supposed to know the history of the country, the fundamentals of its legislation, and the Russian language, it is almost impossible. In such

a situation the simplest and most effective way of acquiring knowledge becomes self-education. At the same time, self-education, like any cognitive activity, is bound up with a whole series of difficulties. All the more so for people who have been cut off from the world of knowledge and who do not possess a certain level of preparation or the skill and capacity for self-instruction, which, unfortunately, is the case with the majority of our young people. For this category of our citizens, easily absorbed, simple instructional materials and textbooks that do not require considerable cognitive effort are needed. Today, in view of the heightened interest in studying the Russian language, in any bookstore one can find a mass of textbooks, manuals, and self-teachers by means of which one can learn the fundamentals of spoken and written speech. Chapter I

ANCIENT AND MEDIEVAL RUS'

The East Slavs in antiquity

The history of the Russian people — the state-forming people of the Russian Federation — has its roots in deep antiquity. The ancestors of the Russians — the ancient Slavs — belong to the Indo-European group of peoples, who from ancient times inhabited the boundless expanses of Eurasia. Many ancient and modern peoples belong to the Indo-Europeans: Armenians, Balts, Germanic peoples, Greeks, Illyrians, Indians, Iranians, Italic peoples, Celts, Slavs, Tocharians, Thracians, Phrygians, and Hittites. They spoke one language and had similar traditions, cultural customs, and beliefs. With the passage of time the Indo-European tribes began to separate from one another, and their common language broke up into many separate languages. At the present time the term “Indo-Europeans” is understood to mean exclusively peoples who speak languages of the Indo-European language family. By the middle of the 2nd millennium BC, the Slavic tribes stood out from the Indo-European community. The first written mentions of the Slavs are found in Roman and Byzantine sources that date to the 1st–2nd centuries AD. These authors call the Slavs the Venedi, the Sclaveni, and the Antes. Speaking of chroniclers, it is appropriate to note that, despite the enormous number of studies devoted to the history of early Rus', certain important milestones and turning points in the initial history of Rus' remain the subject of serious debates. The reason for this is the absence of written sources. The tradition of chronicle-writing in Rus' arose only after the introduction of Christianity. Therefore early information about the Slavs can be found only in the works of foreign authors and in the later works of Russian chroniclers. The principal sources for the study of the early

---------- p. 9 ----------

history of the East Slavs are Byzantine and Eastern — mainly Persian and Arabic — written sources, as well as archaeological, ethnographic, linguistic, and other materials. The most interesting and important information about the first centuries of Russian history is contained in the chronicle compilation the Primary Chronicle (Tale of Bygone Years), compiled by Nestor, a monk of the Kiev Caves Monastery, in the 12th century. The Primary Chronicle remains the most important source for the history of early Rus'. There is probably not a single researcher of Russian history who has not referred to this work. In the 6th–7th centuries AD the Slavs settled widely over the territory from the Black Sea to the Baltic Sea. In the course of this settlement they divided into three branches — the eastern (the ancestors of the Russians, Belarusians, and Ukrainians), the western (the ancestors of the Slovaks, Czechs, Poles, and Lusatian Sorbs), and the southern (the ancestors of the Bulgarians, Serbs, Croats, Slovenes, Macedonians, Bosnians, and Montenegrins). The East Slavs occupied a vast territory from Lake Ilmen in the north to the Black Sea steppes in the south and from the Carpathian Mountains in the west to the Volga in the east. They united in tribal unions. Researchers name about 13 tribes and describe the places of their settlement: in Kiev and on the right bank of the Dnieper — the Polianians (Polans); on the left bank of the Dnieper — the Severians; in the vicinity of the city of Peremyshl on the River San — the White Croats; along the banks of the Pripyat — the Drevlians; on the River Pripyat — the Dregoviches; on the Sozh and Seim rivers — the Radimichs; in the upper reaches of the Volga, the Dnieper, and the Western Dvina — the Krivichs; on Lake Ilmen and the River Volkhov — the Ilmen Slavs; in the interfluve of the Oka and the Volga — the Vyatichs; in the interfluve of the Dniester and the Danube — the Ulichians and the Tivertsi; in the basin of the Southern Bug — the Buzhans and the Volhynians. The largest among these tribal unions were the Polianians (Polans),

---------- p. 10 ----------

who founded the city of Kiev, and the Slavs who founded Novgorod.

The neighbors of the East Slavs in the west were the Baltic peoples and the West Slavs (Poles, Czechs); in the south — the Pechenegs and the Khazars; in the east — the Volga Bulgars and numerous Finno-Ugric tribes (the Mordvins, the Mari, the Muroma). In the 6th–7th centuries the East Slavs lived in clan communities. A clan community was an association of people based on blood kinship — the clan. Each clan had its own territory with arable lands, forest lands, pastures, and bodies of water. The East Slavs lived in small settlements that consisted of 3–5 houses. Their houses were semi-dugouts. Settlements were built along the banks of rivers. The Slavs also built fortified settlements. From some of these fortified settlements, later, in the 9th–10th centuries, cities grew. The Slavs’ chief occupation was agriculture. They also raised livestock and poultry, engaged in fishing, hunted, and gathered the honey of wild bees. They worked the land with the help of the plow. The principal metal from which weapons and tools were made was iron. The East Slavic tribes had already then learned to extract iron from bog ores and to make various articles from it. The East Slavs were pagans, that is, they worshiped many gods. Their chief gods — Perun, Svarog, Dazhdbog, Veles — were connected with agricultural work, fertility, and the phenomena of nature. The gods were represented by images made of stone, wood, and precious metals. As plow agriculture spread and tools were improved, harvests became more abundant and people became prosperous. This gradually led to the appearance of property and social inequality. Clan collectivism also gradually lost its significance. In the 8th–9th centuries the clan community was replaced by the neighboring community — the verv’. The members of the verv’ jointly owned hayfields and forest lands. Arable

---------- p. 11 ----------

lands were divided among individual family households.

The authority of the clan chieftain ceased to operate. All the householders of the district now came together at a general council — the veche. They elected elders to conduct common affairs. Individual communities united into tribes, and the tribes formed unions of tribes. In the 9th century the East Slavs began to conduct trade with Byzantium along the great water route known in history as the route “from the Varangians to the Greeks.” Trade with the Greeks was of very great importance for the East Slavs, but it was bound up with great dangers in view of attacks by marauding bands of Pechenegs. Nomads also attacked the caravan routes along which hunters and beekeepers of the surrounding areas brought their goods to the central tribal settlements for sale to merchants. For the protection of cities and the defense of caravan routes and trading vessels, druzhinas were created. At the head of the druzhina stood a prince, who was elected at the veche. Gradually the right to maintain order in the cities passed to them. Thus local princedoms began to arise — territories subject to one prince or another. By the 9th century signs of statehood in the form of large tribal unions began to appear among the East Slavs. Today the absolute majority of scholars consider that the early Russian state owed its emergence to the large tribal unions. These large associations of tribes, which in the Russian chronicles are called “lands,” were early-state formations. Each such “land” had its own definite territory. These “lands” also had their own princes, their own centers, which in the Russian chronicles are called “grads,” and their own military formations in the form of tribal militias. Another distinctive feature of this epoch is the creation in Rus' of a special military organization. This organization was the so-called “decimal” or “thousand” organization. The warriors of the lands of early Rus' united in tens, hundreds, and thousands, which were headed by desiatskie, sotskie, and tysiatskie. The existence in Rus' of state formations and of their military

---------- p. 12 ----------

might is attested by an impressive event. It took place before the calling of the Varangians. And from it statehood proceeds. This event was the campaign of the Rus-Slavs against the capital of Byzantium on June 18, 860, a week-long siege of it, and the conclusion of a peace treaty between Byzantium and Rus'. This treaty was the first diplomatic success of the nascent Russian statehood and the forerunner of similar agreements that would be concluded in the 10th–11th centuries. Thus, as a result of the socioeconomic and political development of the East Slavic tribes, the conditions took shape for the appearance of a state uniting all the tribes. These circumstances in considerable measure contributed to the fact that in the 9th century the early Russian state was formed on the territory of the East Slavs’ settlement and a new stage in Russian history began.

Kievan Rus' and the first Russian princes

According to the chronicle, in 862 the East Slavic and Finnic-speaking tribes that were under the power of the Varangians ceased to pay them tribute and drove them out. Soon the tribes quarreled — the matter came to armed clashes. Then representatives of all the tribes gathered at a veche and decided to invite familiar Varangians to rule over them. Three brothers responded to the invitation — Rurik, Sineus, and Truvor. Rurik began to reign in Novgorod, Sineus at Beloozero, and Truvor in Izborsk. Two noble warriors of Rurik’s — Askold and Dir — went to Kiev and, having freed the Polianians (Polans) from paying tribute to the Khazars, became their rulers. Two years later Sineus and Truvor died, and Rurik took their regions for himself. Rurik married a girl from a noble Novgorod family, and a son, Igor, was born to him. Thus in the 9th century two large East Slavic associations took shape. One was in the north with its center in Novgorod, the other in the south with its center in Kiev. The chronicle’s information about the calling of the Varangian princes served

---------- p. 13 ----------

as the basis for the creation of the so-called Norman theory of the origin of the early Russian state. Its authors were scholars from Germany, G. Z. Bayer, G. F. Müller, and A. L. Schlözer, who were invited in the 18th century to the St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences. The “Norman” theory asserts that the East Slavs proved incapable of independently creating a state, and that it was brought in from outside. The scholarly unsoundness of the Norman theory is obvious, since states are formed not by the will of individual persons but are the natural result of the prolonged historical development of society. It should be noted that historians who reject the Norman theory of the origin of the early Russian state by no means exclude the presence of elements of truth in the chronicle account of the calling of the Varangians. They consider it quite possible that the Slavs invited several princes with their druzhinas as military leaders, as was done in later times as well, not only in Rus' but also in Western Europe. Historians know for certain that the Russian principalities invited the druzhinas not only of the Varangians but also of their steppe neighbors — the Pechenegs and representatives of other Turkic tribes. Therefore it would be more accurate to say that it was not Norman and not Varangian princes who created the early Russian state, but that they only governed already existing state formations. Prince Oleg. Rurik died in 879, making his kinsman Oleg his successor and leaving in his care his young son Igor. In 882 Oleg, with the aim of expanding his lands, at the head of a fairly large army descended the Dnieper and seized the cities of Smolensk and Liubech and then moved toward Kiev. Having dealt cunningly with the princes ruling there, Askold and Dir, he seized the city of Kiev and made it his capital, saying: “Let this city be the mother of Russian cities.” Oleg succeeded in uniting under his power the most important cities, all the East Slavic, Finno-Ugric, and Baltic tribes, and

---------- p. 14 ----------

also some Turkic tribes, and thereby in creating a single state — Kievan Rus'. Prince Oleg waged successful wars with the Khazars and the Bulgars, and also with the most powerful state of that time — Byzantium. In 907–911, with an enormous army, he made two campaigns with the capture of the capital of Byzantium — Constantinople. The Byzantines were forced to pay tribute and to conclude with Oleg a trade treaty advantageous for the Rus. Prince Oleg died in 912, according to legend from a snakebite. The deeds of the prince are the subject of A. S. Pushkin’s work “The Song of Oleg the Prophet.” Prince Igor. Oleg’s successor was his kinsman — Rurik’s son Igor (years of rule 912–945). Igor successfully fought the Pechenegs, who had appeared by the time of his reign on the southern borders of Rus', and returned the Drevlians, who had separated after Oleg’s death, under the power of Kiev. But the war with Byzantium ended in defeat and in the cancellation of the agreement on duty-free trade of Russian merchants with Byzantium. In the early Russian state, in the course of collecting tribute, the prince with his druzhina rode around his possessions and collected furs, honey, wax, livestock, foodstuffs, and cloth. The population of the principality, especially of recently subjected lands, did not always readily agree to the terms of the tribute. Thus in 945 Igor’s attempt, contrary to the agreement, to collect additional tribute from the Drevlian tribe ended in his death. Princess Olga. After Igor’s death in 945, power passed to his son Sviatoslav. However, Sviatoslav was then four years old, and therefore Igor’s wife and Sviatoslav’s mother, Olga, took the government of the principality into her own hands. She cruelly suppressed the uprising and, acting farsightedly, in order to avoid rebellions, introduced clear rules for the collection of tribute. Thus a new form of collecting poliudie was established — the so-called povoz. Princess Olga, with the aim of stopping the raids of external enemies, carried out a number of important reforms. She also succeeded in the arrangement

---------- p. 15 ----------

of the country — she built fortified cities and improved the organization of landed economy. The first stone buildings in Kiev were constructed under Princess Olga. She also strengthened international ties with Byzantium. In 957 the princess made a visit to Constantinople. The chronicles report that the Greeks received her with honor. Princess Olga entered the history of Rus' as the first ruler to accept the Christian faith. She died in 969, bequeathing that she be buried according to the Christian rite. The Russian Orthodox Church in 1547 numbered Princess Olga among the saints. Prince Sviatoslav (942–972).1 The son of Igor and Olga, Sviatoslav became famous in history as one of the greatest military commanders of Rus'. He did much to defend the Russian lands from the invasion of nomads and to establish trade with other countries. As a result of numerous military campaigns, Sviatoslav succeeded in subjecting the lands of the Vyatichs, won a victory over Volga Bulgaria, smashed the Khazar Khaganate, conquered the Principality of Tmutarakan and the Mordvin tribes, and drove off the Pechenegs. However, in the struggle with Byzantium he suffered defeat and only with difficulty obtained the conclusion of a peace treaty. Prince Sviatoslav, several years before his death, divided Rus' among his three sons: Yaropolk, Oleg, and Vladimir. In 972, while the prince was absent, the Pechenegs again came to the Kievan lands and nearly seized the capital. Sviatoslav and his druzhina fell into an ambush arranged by the Pechenegs at the instigation of the Byzantines, and perished in the struggle with them. Tradition has it that from Sviatoslav’s skull the Pecheneg khan Kurya made a cup decorated with gold and precious stones, believing that in this way the strength, daring, and bravery of the Russian commander would pass to him. 1 Here and below, the years in parentheses after a name indicate the years of rule.

The flowering of Kievan Rus'

The chief questions in the activity of the Kievan princes were the unification of all the East Slavic tribes, the organization of the defense of the state’s borders, and the establishment of advantageous trade relations with other countries. It was precisely these tasks that were solved in the years of the rule of Vladimir Sviatoslavich. And in the period of the reign of Yaroslav the Wise the early Russian state reached its flowering. Prince Vladimir (980–1015). Immediately after the death of Sviatoslav a fierce struggle for possession of the Kievan throne flared up among his sons, which ended in the victory of Vladimir, the third son of Sviatoslav. During his rule Prince Vladimir succeeded in doing much to expand and strengthen the territory of the state. He subjected to his power the lands of the Vyatichs, the Radimichs, and the Yatvingians, and thereby completed the process of unifying all the East Slavic tribes; he successfully fought Volga Bulgaria, defeated Poland in war, and annexed several of its cities to Rus'; for successful struggle with the nomads he created an effective defensive system in the form of fortresses connected with one another by high ramparts and deep ditches. Having given up distant military campaigns, Vladimir devoted himself to the arrangement of the state. He abolished the tribal principalities and subordinated them to newly formed princely centers, in which he appointed his twelve sons as governors of the Kievan prince. Under Vladimir, for the first time in Rus', they began to mint their own money — gold and silver coins. Vladimir conducted an active foreign policy. During his rule many treaties were concluded with the countries of Europe — Hungary, Poland, Bohemia, Byzantium, and the Papal States. With the aim of establishing closer interstate relations, Prince Vladimir became related by marriage to the ruling houses

---------- p. 17 ----------

of neighboring states: for his eldest son, Sviatopolk, he arranged a match with a Polish princess, and he married another son, Yaroslav, to the daughter of the king of Sweden. The adoption of Christianity. The greatest historical merit of Prince Vladimir is the introduction of Christianity in Rus'. A single religion contributed to the unification of all the East Slavic and Finno-Ugric tribes into a single early Russian nationality. With the adoption of Christianity, Kievan Rus' entered the circle of the Christian countries of Europe and joined in the achievements of ancient culture. Writing began to spread in Rus', stone architecture was born, and the arts of icon painting and fresco painting arose. The Church became not only a religious organization but also a center of education and of the moral upbringing of society. In monasteries chronicle-writing and book production were conducted, and schools were opened. Christianity produced a fundamental upheaval in the social and family life of the Russian people: it forbade blood feud and sacrifices, displacing such survivals of paganism as polygamy, the purchase of wives, and other practices that humiliated the dignity of woman. It also contributed to the appearance of laws improving the social and family position of women. Thus Prince Vladimir, by introducing Christianity, opened a new era in the history of the Russian people and laid a firm spiritual foundation for its further development. For these merits the Church numbered him among the saints. And the Russian people expressed its love for the great prince in the affectionate nickname Vladimir the Fair Sun. Prince Vladimir died on July 15, 1015, leaving behind him a good memory among the people. Yaroslav the Wise (1019–1054) came to the Kievan throne after a prolonged and bloody internecine struggle for supreme power, in the course of which his brother Sviatopolk killed his brothers Boris, Gleb, and Sviatoslav.

---------- p. 18 ----------

The period of the rule of Yaroslav the Wise is a time of cultural upsurge and of the military might of Kievan Rus'. Continuing the work of his father, Yaroslav built new defensive fortifications and finally smashed the Pechenegs. Having carried out military campaigns in the Baltic region, the Polish-Lithuanian lands, and Byzantium, he expanded the territory of Rus' still further. Now it stretched from the Taman Peninsula to the Northern Dvina and from the Dniester and the Vistula to the upper reaches of the Volga and the Don. In the years of the rule of Yaroslav the Wise the most important legal document was compiled — Russkaya Pravda, which regulated questions of the relations between the population and the authorities and of criminal, family, and inheritance law. Russkaya Pravda is a most valuable source for the study of the political and socioeconomic system of Kievan Rus'. Under Yaroslav the Wise the international ties of Rus' with the countries of Europe developed actively. This was facilitated, in particular, by dynastic marriages, by means of which the grand prince became related to the ruling houses of Hungary, Byzantium, Germany, Norway, Poland, and France. Yaroslav did much for the development of education and culture. Under him many schools were opened, libraries were created, and beautiful churches were built. He himself was a very educated and enlightened man, knew several foreign languages, and loved to read books, as the chronicles testify, “often both by night and by day.” It was not for nothing that he was nicknamed “the Wise.” In order to avoid internecine strife and the struggle for power among his descendants, Yaroslav before his death divided the state among three sons: Iziaslav received Kiev, Sviatoslav received Chernigov, and Vsevolod received Pereyaslavl. At the same time he established a new order of succession to the throne, according to which supreme power passed to the eldest in the clan, and not to the eldest son, as had been the case before. Yaroslav the Wise died — in 1054 — at the height of his power and fame.

Rus' on the path to fragmentation

Yaroslav the Wise, having before his death divided the state among his sons, bequeathed them to live in peace and harmony. However, they followed their father’s precepts only for a short time, and after the passage of about a decade and a half they entered upon a series of endless bloody discords in which Yaroslav’s grandsons were already taking part. The internecine wars for the throne continued even at the time when a new enemy appeared on the borders of the state — the Cumans (Polovtsians) — a nomadic Turkic people. The devastating raids of the Cumans on the Russian lands continued right up to the Mongol invasion of Rus'. Vladimir Monomakh (1113–1125). It was precisely in that period, when internecine wars and the unsuccessful struggle with the nomads had brought Kievan Rus' to the brink of disintegration, that Prince Vladimir Monomakh, who was ruling in Pereyaslavl, came onto the political stage. Vladimir was a grandson of Yaroslav the Wise. And he was nicknamed Monomakh after the family name of his grandfather on his mother’s side — the Byzantine emperor Constantine Monomachos. In the princely clan Vladimir Monomakh enjoyed enormous authority. In a situation in which many Russian princes did not recognize the seniority of Sviatopolk Iziaslavich, who was ruling in Kiev, Vladimir Monomakh was the figure who by his authority united a large part of the fragmented Russian principalities. Vladimir Monomakh subdued the princes and called them to unity. He could punish those of his relatives who entered into collusion with the Cumans and started internecine strife. On Monomakh’s initiative, in 1097 a congress of princes was held in the city of Liubech. The participants in the congress came to an agreement that each of them would become the owner of the lands that had come to him by inheritance and would not seek to seize the possessions of his relatives. It was decided to conduct the struggle against the Cumans jointly. But the princes had not yet had time to disperse to their appanages when internecine strife flared up with new

---------- p. 20 ----------

force. Nevertheless, Vladimir Monomakh more than once organized campaigns of the Russian druzhinas against the Cumans, inflicting sensitive blows upon them. Thanks to three large campaigns in 1103, 1107, and 1111, in the course of which crushing defeats were inflicted on the enemy, the nomads ceased their raids on Russian cities for many years. The death of Sviatopolk coincided with a powerful popular uprising in Kiev. Debtors driven to despair by the arbitrary conduct of moneylenders smashed the houses of the rich, of merchants, and of large feudal lords. In violation of the “ladder” system of the transfer of power, the Kievan nobility invited Vladimir Monomakh to govern Kiev. The further course of events showed that in the complex political situation in which Kievan Rus' found itself, the invitation to the grand princely throne of the experienced, intelligent, and decisive political figure and military commander Vladimir Monomakh was an absolutely correct decision. Having come to the Kievan throne, Monomakh not only suppressed the revolt but also, with the aim of preventing similar popular disturbances, put in order at the legislative level the norms of usurious exactions. These norms were reflected in the Statute of Vladimir Monomakh. The Statute also forbade the established rules according to which a debtor was turned into a slave. The Statute of Vladimir Monomakh, as yet another part of Russkaya Pravda, marked a new important stage in the development of the legislative foundation of the state. The time of the rule of Vladimir Monomakh became the period of the last flowering and might of Kievan Rus' as a single state. Under his skillful rule a considerable part of the early Russian state was united. Princely wars ceased. Vladimir Monomakh was not only a skillful, wise, and educated politician but also a talented writer. In his work the Instruction he gives his children the precept to live in peace and to help the destitute, the weak, widows, and orphans. To the pen of the grand prince

---------- p. 21 ----------

belong two more works — the “Letter to Oleg Sviatoslavich” and the “Prayer.” Vladimir Monomakh died in 1125, leaving the grand princely throne to his son Mstislav, whom the chroniclers called the Great. Under him the unity of the state as a whole was preserved, but after his death, in 1132, Kievan Rus' broke up into many principalities and lands.

The disintegration of Kievan Rus'

After the death of Mstislav the Great the Kievan throne was occupied by the eldest in the clan of the Monomakhovichi — Yaropolk Vladimirovich. It seemed that everything was taking shape according to the order of succession to grand princely power by seniority. However, many princes in separate branches of the clan of Yaroslav the Wise were not satisfied with such an order. A fierce struggle for power unfolded among them. In the course of an almost ten-year internecine war the Kievan throne passed several times from one prince to another. But none of them was able to bring the country peace and unity. Thus, at the beginning of the 1130s, Kievan Rus' entered a period of rapid disintegration into separate lands and principalities. If in the middle of the 12th century the number of principalities that did not submit to Kiev was 15, then by the end of the 12th century they already numbered 50. The rulers of these principalities, each of which in size exceeded an average European state of that time, conducted an absolutely independent foreign and domestic policy, had military druzhinas, adopted laws, and entered into trade relations with other countries. Among the multitude of independent lands, the strongest political centers — states of a kind — were the Vladimir-Suzdal, Galicia–Volhynia, and Novgorod lands.

---------- p. 22 ----------

The Vladimir-Suzdal principality

This principality occupied the largest part — almost a third of the territory — of the broken-up Kievan Rus' and was located in the northeastern part of the Russian lands, in the interfluve of the Oka and the Volga. The power of the Vladimir princes had the features of a monarchy, and the sole ruler of the principality was the prince. It was precisely the Vladimir-Suzdal principality that subsequently became the center of the unification of the Russian lands and the cradle of the formation of the Great Russian nationality. The political history of the principality begins in 1125, when the founder of the local princely dynasty, Prince Yuri Dolgoruky (1090–1157) — the youngest son of Vladimir Monomakh — declared the independence of the Vladimir-Suzdal principality from Kiev. Yuri received the nickname Dolgoruky for his active participation in battles on various outskirts of Rus'. Prince Dolgoruky was not only a skillful military commander but also a talented city-builder. In the years of his rule many fortress-cities were built (Yuryev-Polsky, Dmitrov, Zvenigorod, Pereyaslavl-Zalessky, Ksnyatin), as well as villages, churches, and monasteries. The first chronicle mention of Moscow is connected with the name of Prince Yuri Dolgoruky. According to another record, in 1156 the prince had Moscow enclosed with strong oak walls. With the aim of unifying Rus', in 1157 he seized Kiev, but soon died. After the death of Yuri Dolgoruky the reign passed to his son Andrei Bogolyubsky (1110–1174), nicknamed Bogolyubsky for his deep veneration of the Vladimir Icon of the Mother of God. In 1169 he conquered the Kievan throne and transferred the capital of Rus' to the city of Vladimir. The unifying policy of Andrei Bogolyubsky, which he conducted among the princes, cost him his life. In 1174 the prince was killed as a result of a boyar conspiracy. The third son of Yuri Dolgoruky, Vsevolod the Big Nest (1176–1216), occupied the princely throne, having won in the internecine

---------- p. 23 ----------

struggle for power that began immediately after Andrei’s death. He received the nickname “the Big Nest” for his large progeny — he had eight sons and four daughters. Having defeated the boyars in the struggle for sole power, Vsevolod preserved the monarchy in his principality. The years of his rule became a period of flowering for the Vladimir-Suzdal principality. After the death of Vsevolod the Big Nest in 1216, the single strong principality, because of internecine wars, fell apart into several parts.

The Principality of Galicia–Volhynia

In the southwest of Rus' at the end of the 12th century the Principality of Galicia–Volhynia was formed. Before this the Galician and Volhynian lands had been independent formations. Their unification took place in 1199 under Roman Mstislavich, a great-great-grandson of Vladimir Monomakh. The mild climate and fertile soils of these places contributed to the development of plow agriculture and to the early appearance of large feudal landholding. Various crafts had long been widespread here. Trade routes leading to the countries of Europe passed through the territories of the principality. In addition, the lands of the principality were not subjected to raids by nomads. All this contributed to the development of agricultural and craft production, to active foreign trade, and, as a consequence, to the growth and prosperity of cities and villages. The largest cities of the Principality of Galicia–Volhynia were Berestye, Vladimir-Volynsky, Galich, Dorogobuzh, Peremyshl, Terebovl, and Kholm. In 1203 Roman Mstislavich ascended the Kievan throne and received the title of grand prince. Then he united Southern and Southwestern Rus'. The Principality of Galicia–Volhynia turned into an enormous state, equal in the size of its territory to the Roman Empire of that time. After the death of Roman Mstislavich in 1205 the principality

---------- p. 24 ----------

broke up into small appanages. A prolonged internecine war began. Only after the passage of more than thirty years did Roman Mstislavich’s grown son, Daniel, succeed in again uniting the Galician and Volhynian lands. In 1240 he took possession of Kiev and began to restore the principality within its former limits. But this process was interrupted by the Mongol invasion.

The Novgorod Land

The Novgorod Land was located in the northwest of Rus' and occupied a vast territory from the Gulf of Finland to the Ural Mountains and from the Arctic Ocean to the upper reaches of the Volga. Besides Novgorod proper, it included such cities as Bezhetsk, Vologda, Volok on the Lama, Vyatka, Ladoga, Rusa, Pskov, and Torzhok. The Novgorod Land was one of the richest and most economically developed territories of Rus'. The chief occupation of the population was agriculture, but the economic might of Novgorod and its wealth were based on crafts and trade. Novgorod conducted active trade both with other Russian lands and with Europe. The principal items of export were furs, leather, honey, flax, whale and walrus oil, wax, pitch, and the articles of craftsmen. In Kievan Rus', by tradition, Novgorod was held by that one of the princes who held the Kievan throne. At the beginning of the appanage period — in 1136 — the local boyars emerged victorious in the struggle for power. An original political system was established in Novgorod. The originality of this system consisted in the fact that supreme power belonged to the veche — the people’s assembly. At the veche the city’s officials were elected — the posadnik, the tysiatsky, and the archimandrite. The posadnik was the head of the city administration. He was elected from among the boyars. He decided questions of administration, headed the court, appointed and removed officials, and directed foreign policy. The tysiatsky headed the tax system and the people’s militia.

---------- p. 25 ----------

The archimandrite, besides being the head of the Church and of the church court, kept the treasury and took part in the conduct of foreign policy. The prince in Novgorod was not considered the supreme authority. He was invited to perform the role of military commander. The veche concluded a treaty with the prince in which all his rights and duties were provided for. Any violation of the terms of the treaty ended in the expulsion of the prince. Lord Novgorod the Great — as the Novgorodians loftily named their state — despite the striving of neighboring principalities and foreign invaders to subject this unique city to themselves, preserved its independence. And, having existed for three and a half centuries in unchanged form, in the last quarter of the 15th century it entered the composition of the Russian state, becoming its integral part. Feudal fragmentation, being on the whole a negative factor in the development of Kievan Rus', at the same time contributed to the accelerated growth of cities, the improvement of crafts, and the growth of trade. Since each prince strove to strengthen and arrange his own principality, conditions were created for an influx of population from other places, and crafts, trade, and various kinds of extractive activity were encouraged. The increase of boyar and monastic votchinas contributed to the expansion of arable lands. As a result, peasant households, craft production, popular crafts, and trade began to develop at a rapid pace. Gradually cities became not only political and economic but also cultural centers. The disintegration of Kievan Rus', although it slowed the process of the further consolidation of the early Russian community, yet the unity of faith, language, and culture and the common historical roots contributed to the preservation of the already formed early Russian nationality. However, the continuing process of political fragmentation and princely internecine strife weakened the country’s defensive capacity in the face of the approaching external threat.

Invasions of foreign conquerors into the Russian lands

The 13th century became for the early Russian state the beginning of great trials. From the east Mongol hordes invaded Rus', and from the northwest — German, Danish, and Swedish crusaders. Only the heroic struggle against external enemies allowed Rus' to preserve the conditions for independent development. The most destructive for the land of early Rus' proved to be the invasion of the Mongol conquerors.

The Mongol invasion and the vassal dependence of Rus'

At the beginning of the 13th century, in the steppes of Central Asia, as a result of the unification of numerous related nomadic tribes, the state of the Mongols was formed. After the name of one of the tribes, the Chinese called all the inhabitants of Mongolia Tatars. Subsequently the Russians, having borrowed this name from the Chinese, began to call them the Mongol-Tatars. At a gathering of Mongol khans in 1206 the cruel military leader Temuchin became the ruler of the Mongol state, and at that time received the name Genghis Khan — the great khan. The foundation of Genghis Khan’s policy became campaigns of conquest. Having created a numerous army, in a short time he seized Southern Siberia, Northern China, Korea, Tibet, Central Asia, Iran, and Transcaucasia. In the course of these campaigns of conquest hundreds of cities were smashed and burned, hundreds of thousands of people were exterminated, and many outstanding monuments of culture of various peoples were destroyed. The conquerors brought to the subjected peoples devastation, poverty, violence, and slavery. Such a fate now threatened the countries of Eastern Europe, including the Russian lands. The troops of Genghis Khan, after a predatory campaign in Transcaucasia,

---------- p. 27 ----------

invaded the Cuman steppes bordering on Rus'. The Cumans turned to the Russian princes for help. Only 15 princes responded to the request. The battle between the joint Russian-Cuman army and the Mongols took place on May 31, 1223, on the River Kalka. The Russian warriors fought courageously and bravely, but because of the lack of coordination in the princes’ actions and the flight of the Cumans from the field of battle this battle was lost. The Russians suffered heavy losses — in the slaughter 6 Russian princes perished, and of the ordinary warriors only a tenth part survived. After the battle on the Kalka the Mongol hordes did not advance into the depths of Rus' but returned to the Mongol steppes. In 1235 a kurultai of Mongol khans adopted a decision on a campaign against Rus'. This campaign was headed by Batu Khan, a grandson of Genghis Khan, who had by that time died. In 1237 Mongol hosts invaded Northeastern Rus'. The first principality that the Mongols attacked was the Ryazan principality. Despite the strong and stubborn resistance of the Russian druzhinas and the local militia, the invaders succeeded in breaking into the city and burning it to the ground. The local population was exterminated or led away into captivity. After Ryazan, in a short time such cities as Kolomna, Moscow, Vladimir, Suzdal, Yaroslavl, Tver, and Kostroma were seized and devastated. Thus in 1238 all of Northeastern Rus' found itself under the power of the Mongols. After a certain respite, in 1239, Batu Khan undertook a new campaign — this time against Southern and Southwestern Rus'. Kiev was seized and plundered, and the Principality of Galicia–Volhynia was devastated. Having smashed Southern Rus', the Mongol hordes in the spring of 1241 invaded Poland, Hungary, Bohemia, Moravia, and reached Croatia. Despite their successes, the invaders were forced to stop, since they were not receiving reinforcements. The conquerors began to withdraw to the east. By the end of 1242 they had reached the steppes of the Lower Volga. Here the Mongols founded their state, known

---------- p. 28 ----------

in history under the name the Golden Horde. Its capital became the city of

Sarai — modern Astrakhan.

The Golden Horde was part of the Mongol Empire, and in the 1280s, as a result of the weakening of the power of the all-Mongol khan, it became an independent state. The Horde included the Lower Volga region, the Caspian steppes, part of Central Asia, the North Caucasus, Crimea, and Western Siberia. Rus' did not enter the composition of the Golden Horde. However, being in a state of fragmentation and weakened by the raids of the invaders, the Russian lands were forced to recognize vassal dependence on the Mongols. The Russian princes retained power within the limits of their principalities, but their activity and the life of the Russian lands were under the constant control of the baskaks — special representatives of the supreme ruler of the Horde. No Russian prince could enter upon a reign without receiving a yarlyk of the Mongol khan. A yarlyk was the name given to a special document conferring the right to reign. For the princes the most attractive was the Golden Yarlyk, which gave the right to the grand reign of Vladimir. Since, beginning with the second half of the 13th century, Vladimir was becoming the political center of Rus', a prince who received a yarlyk for it received the status of “chief” prince. Therefore the grand reign of Vladimir was an object of sharp rivalry among princely dynasties. Since the Mongol khans feared most of all the unification of Russian forces, they skillfully used the issuance of yarlyks to intensify discord among the princes. And they thus created obstacles on the path of the unification of the Russian principalities and the centralization of Rus'. The chief form of the economic dependence of Rus' on the Horde was the payment of tribute. The entire population of the Russian lands was obliged regularly to pay tribute to the Mongols. Initially the tribute was collected by the Mongols themselves, who often robbed the people. This caused numerous popular uprisings in a number of Russian lands. Then the khans entrusted the collection of tribute to the Russian princes. From all taxes and duties only

---------- p. 29 ----------

the Orthodox clergy was exempted. The Church even received from the Horde special yarlyks ensuring its rights to church property. Church lands were protected by Mongol officials, but during raids they were also subjected to devastation. Mongol influence, which lasted 240 years, led Rus' to political, economic, sociodemographic, and cultural decline. Many Russian lands and cities were devastated, and entire principalities were destroyed. According to archaeological data, more than two-thirds of the cities of early Rus' were completely destroyed, and almost a third of them ceased to exist altogether. One-tenth of the population perished. Mongol dominion aggravated political fragmentation. If at the moment of the Mongol invasion there were about 80 appanage principalities in Rus', then by the beginning of the 14th century their number had passed 400. This was one of the principal reasons for the political and economic lag of Rus' behind the developed countries of Europe. Serious demographic changes took place. The population of the country declined sharply, especially the population of the cities. A multitude of people were killed or led away into captivity. The death of many princes, members of the druzhinas, city-builders, and craftsmen severely undermined the gene pool of the Great Russian people. Colossal damage was inflicted on agriculture — the leading branch of the early Russian economy. Agricultural centers fell into decline, arable lands were abandoned, and the established system of agriculture was disrupted. The conquerors caused enormous damage to the culture of Rus'. Many monuments of culture were destroyed and plundered. In the fires perished churches, monasteries, the most valuable objects of art, and chronicles. As a result of the death or the forcible leading away into the Horde of skilled masters and experienced craftsmen, such complex kinds of craft as the smelting and working of iron and

---------- p. 30 ----------

steel, the working of nonferrous metals, glass production, stonecutting, and many others disappeared for a long time. Stone construction came to a halt, and the pictorial, applied, and decorative arts and chronicle-writing fell into decline. The traditional political, economic, and cultural relations of Rus' with foreign countries were considerably weakened. Many historians are united in the opinion that it was precisely thanks to the heroic struggle of the peoples of Rus' for liberation, which did not cease for a single day and wore out the forces of the invaders, that the countries of Central and Western Europe were saved from Mongol conquest.

The Russian people’s struggle against Western aggression

Almost simultaneously with the Mongol invasion a threat arose from the West. Already at the beginning of the 13th century a considerable part of the Eastern Baltic — the territories of present-day Estonia and Latvia — had been seized by German crusader knights. The Germans called this territory Livonia, after the Livs — one of the Baltic tribes. Here they created the Livonian Brothers of the Sword. In the lands of the Eastern Baltic, besides the Livs, lived the Latgalians, the Curonians, the Semigallians, and the Ests. All these tribes were pagans. The conquerors, under the pretext of bringing the local population to Christianity, cruelly suppressed them. With the aim of seizing new territories, in 1237 the Livonian Brothers of the Sword united with the Teutonic Order, sent by the Roman Catholic Church. This united organization was called the Livonian Order in Rus'. The Livonian Order was an instrument of the Vatican’s colonial policy in Eastern Europe.
